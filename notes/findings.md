# Findings

Running notes from the replication and the two evaluations. Numbers are held-out
SimLex-999 Spearman unless stated otherwise.

## The original RoBERTa results reproduce

Two replicates, four modes, 8000 steps each, run from the original code on our GPU with
the datasets regenerated by its own scripts.

| Mode | ours | original |
|---|---|---|
| baseline (step 0) | 0.570 | 0.557 |
| vanilla_all | 0.560 | 0.549 |
| vanilla_targetwords | 0.571 | 0.558 |
| rsr_all (templates) | 0.627 | 0.611 |
| rsr_targetwords (real sentences) | 0.477 | 0.486 |

Everything lands ~0.013 above the original, which is almost certainly the corpus - our
Wikipedia stream collected 405k sentences against the original 336k. Ordering and
conclusions are identical. The fMRI pipeline reproduced exactly too: 2,296
voxels, same as originally reported.

## The effect decays at our learning rate - but not at the original one

The decay experiment showed the late decline is not caused by RSR pressure, so
the obvious next question was what happens if we simply keep training. One run at
layer 12, weighted 0.7, constant lambda, 4000 steps, evaluated every 200, seed 1,
strict pool - five times our usual length and half of the original 8000.

| step | Neither gain | All-pairs gain |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | +0.085 | +0.042 |
| 400 | +0.085 | +0.027 |
| 600 | +0.059 | -0.002 |
| 1200 | +0.039 | -0.013 |
| 2000 | +0.029 | -0.018 |
| 3000 | +0.025 | -0.021 |
| 4000 | +0.032 | -0.017 |

Two things happen, and they are different from each other.

**All pairs crosses zero at about step 600 and settles near -0.017.** Trained long
enough, RSR ends up *worse* than its own MLM control on general SimLex. That is
the collapse reported in the original work: the real-sentence run there sits below its control on All
pairs at step 8000. We reproduce it - we had simply never trained far enough to
see it.

**Neither decays from +0.085 to a plateau around +0.025 and stops.** It keeps
roughly a third of the peak effect and holds it from step 2000 out to 4000. So
the unsupervised gain is not purely transient, but most of it is.

The control is flat throughout at 0.52-0.53 on Neither, so this is the RSR arm
falling, not the baseline rising.

**What this means for the disagreement with the original result - with a caveat added later.**
The first reading was that we are looking at the same curve at different points
on the x-axis: the original reports endpoints at 8000 steps, we report a best checkpoint
near step 200-400, and at that training length our own numbers look like the original ones.
The learning-rate run below complicates that, because the all-pairs collapse
turns out to be specific to our high learning rate and does not appear at the original one.
What survives is the weaker and still useful statement: RSR installs this
structure early and continued training erodes it, with the erosion hitting the
general pairs hardest and the unsupervised pairs least.

That is a better claim than the one we had. It is more specific, it explains a
disagreement rather than disputing it, and it makes the short training run a
finding rather than a convenience. It does mean the headline needs stating with
its training length attached: +0.070 at 800 steps early-stopped, decaying to
about +0.025 if training continues.

Caveats, and they are larger than they first looked. This is one seed, the
trajectory is visibly noisy in the plateau region - Neither gain bounces between
+0.007 and +0.041 between steps 2200 and 4000 - and the section below shows that
run-to-run divergence at a *fixed* seed reaches about 0.015 by step 2000. So
"about +0.025" is a level with an uncertainty of the same order as itself. The
qualitative shape - rise, peak, decay, partial plateau - reproduces across runs
and is safe to state. The specific plateau value is not. Three seeds at 4000
steps would be needed to quote it, at roughly six hours.

Run: `results/bert_continued_*_20260826_022741.csv`.

## The effect is not specific to SimLex - it transfers to MEN

Everything we report rests on held-out SimLex-999, partitioned by our supervision
vocabulary, which invites the question of whether we have found something about
RSR or something about SimLex. MEN answers it cheaply: 2,980 of its 3,000 pairs
use words we already have harvested sentences for, so this is evaluation only, on
the existing strict-pool checkpoints.

MEN is also the sharpest available test because it rates **relatedness**, not
similarity - which is exactly why it was excluded from the supervision pool.
Similarity and relatedness come apart on pairs like coffee/cup.

Spearman rho, partitioned by our supervision vocabulary:

| partition | n | vanilla | MLM control | RSR | RSR - control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| all | 2980 | 0.7305 | 0.7305 | 0.7933 | +0.063 |
| both in RSR | 294 | 0.7429 | 0.7532 | 0.7803 | +0.027 |
| one in RSR | 1117 | 0.7221 | 0.7209 | 0.7959 | +0.075 |
| neither in RSR | 1569 | 0.7256 | 0.7241 | 0.7852 | +0.061 |

**The effect transfers.** +0.061 on unsupervised pairs, against +0.070 on SimLex,
on a benchmark that was never used for training or model selection and whose
vocabulary only partly overlaps ours.

**The MLM control buys nothing here.** Vanilla and control are identical on all
pairs, 0.7305 against 0.7305. So the entire movement is RSR's.

**The generalisation signature replicates.** Both in RSR - the pairs that
actually carried training ratings - gains least, +0.027. That is the same
counterintuitive pattern as SimLex, now on independent data with 1,569
unsupervised pairs.

A prediction that failed, worth recording. Going in, the expectation was that RSR
would *not* help relatedness, since it is trained on similarity, and that this
would demonstrate specificity. It helps nearly as much as it helps similarity. So
the defensible claim is the broader one: RSR improves lexical representation
quality generally, and that shows up whatever benchmark measures it. That is
simpler than a specificity story and it also fits the brain result, which is a
third kind of measurement entirely.

Caveat: one checkpoint (seed 1), the same limitation as brain and GLUE. Pair
counts are large but pairs are not independent samples of the model, so the n
column should not be read as statistical power over training runs.

WordSim-353 was considered as a second transfer benchmark but only 31 percent of
its pairs have harvested sentences, too thin once partitioned.

Script: `experiments/rsr_pretraining/eval_men_transfer.py`.

## The original configuration, run on our code: length and lr are ruled out

The decisive experiment. BERT, layer 12, weighted 0.7, **learning rate 1e-5 for
8000 steps** - the original learning rate and training length, ten times our usual run
- on the strict pool, real sentences, evaluated every 400 steps against its own
matched control. One seed.

If training length or learning rate explained why the original real-sentence RSR is flat
and ours is not, our gain should decay toward it. It does not.

| partition | peak | at step 8000 | mean, last 2000 steps |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neither in RSR | +0.0945 @1200 | +0.0666 | +0.0715 |
| All pairs | +0.0469 @800 | -0.0019 | +0.0047 |
| Both in RSR | - | -0.0624 | -0.0610 |

**Neither holds at +0.067 after 8000 steps**, averaging +0.072 across the last
2000. That is essentially our headline number, sustained at the original settings. The original
equivalent is +0.004. So neither training length nor learning rate explains the
disagreement, and both can be struck off the list.

Two further results here are less comfortable and should be reported rather than
buried.

**All pairs decays to zero.** It ends at -0.002. The general SimLex advantage is
genuinely transient; only the unsupervised partition survives long training. This
is consistent with the corpus check, which already showed all-pairs was the row
that would not hold up.

**The supervised pairs degrade badly.** Both in RSR falls throughout and finishes
at -0.062 against its control. Trained long enough, RSR makes the pairs it was
given ratings for materially *worse*. That is a real cost of the method. It is
also the half of the original pattern that we do reproduce - its Both drops hard with
real sentences too.

So the picture at long training is a dissociation: the pairs RSR was trained on
get worse, the general set returns to baseline, and only the pairs it was never
supervised on retain a gain. That is a stronger and stranger version of the
central claim than we had, and it is the thing worth raising.

**What is left to explain the disagreement.** Four differences remain untested:
the original uses RoBERTa where we use BERT; it reads from layer 5 where we read from 12;
it alternates the two losses where we combine them each step; and it augments
supervision with morphological variants, so its partitions are drawn over
different pairs than ours. The cheapest next test is layer 5 - one flag, about an
hour.

Caveat: one seed, and same-seed divergence reaches about 0.015 by step 2000, so
the late curve is a shape rather than a set of values. The qualitative
conclusion - the gain persists instead of decaying to zero - is far larger than
that uncertainty.

Run: `results/bert_continued_*_20260826_055015.csv`.

## How far can a single run be trusted? Not as far at 8000 steps

Two runs with the *same* seed and the same learning rate, differing only in total
step count (2000 against 8000), give a free measurement of run-to-run
reproducibility, because their trajectories should coincide:

| step | 2000-step run | 8000-step run | divergence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 400 | 0.5954 | 0.5956 | 0.0002 |
| 800 | 0.6052 | 0.6079 | 0.0027 |
| 1200 | 0.6043 | 0.6074 | 0.0031 |
| 1600 | 0.5967 | 0.6056 | 0.0089 |
| 2000 | 0.5795 | 0.5949 | 0.0154 |

Divergence grows steadily with training length. The 0.0027 at step 800 matches
the 0.003 floor measured independently from the multiloss and layer12 control
arms, so this is the same GPU nondeterminism amplifying through training rather
than anything new.

The practical consequence is a rule for reading our own numbers. **At the length
we actually report - best checkpoint near step 200-400 - single-seed runs are
reproducible to about 0.0002 and the 5-seed headline is solid.** At step 2000 a
single run carries roughly 0.015 of uncertainty, which is half the size of the
effect still present there. So every long-run conclusion in these notes should be
read as a shape rather than a set of values, and anything quantitative past about
step 1000 needs several seeds before it is quoted.

This is worth raising unprompted rather than being caught by it. It also explains
why the plateau region of the 4000-step run looks so ragged: that raggedness is
not the model oscillating, it is the measurement floor rising.

## The original learning rate makes our result stronger, not weaker

The obvious follow-up: is the peak an artefact of our learning rate? Ours is
5e-5, the original is 1e-5. One run at lr 1e-5, layer 12, weighted 0.7, 2000 steps,
evaluated every 200, seed 1, strict pool. Gains against its own matched control:

| step | lr 1e-5 Neither | all pairs | lr 5e-5 Neither | all pairs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 200 | +0.061 | +0.026 | +0.085 | +0.042 |
| 800 | +0.090 | +0.045 | +0.071 | +0.011 |
| 1200 | +0.094 | +0.047 | +0.039 | -0.013 |
| 2000 | +0.072 | +0.018 | +0.029 | -0.018 |

Three things follow.

**The peak is not a learning-rate artefact.** At a fifth of the learning rate the
Neither gain reaches +0.094, slightly *higher* than the +0.085 we get at 5e-5. If
anyone argues the effect only appears because we train hot, this is the answer.

**The learning rate sets the timescale.** The peak moves from step 200 to step
1200, roughly preserving the product of learning rate and steps.

**The all-pairs collapse is specific to the high learning rate.** At 5e-5 the
all-pairs gain crosses zero by step 600 and settles near -0.017. At 1e-5 it never
goes negative at all within 2000 steps - it is still +0.018 at the end.

That last point matters, because the original runs train at 1e-5 and *do* collapse on all
pairs. So the collapse we produced at 5e-5 is not the same phenomenon as the original one, and
learning rate alone does not explain the disagreement. It is worth being careful
here: the decay is real at both learning rates, but the specific all-pairs
collapse is not evidence that we reproduce the original result.

At 1e-5 our gain was still decaying at step 2000, so it might have reached the original
flat result by 8000. It does not - see the next section, which settles it.

Runs: `results/bert_continued_*_20260826_044036.csv`.

## Decaying the RSR weight does not help, and rules out a hypothesis

McClure & Kriegeskorte (2015), "Representational Distance Learning for Deep
Neural Networks" (arXiv 1511.03979), is the closest methodological ancestor of
RSR: they drive a student network's RDMs toward a teacher's, on vision tasks,
and note it could take brain measurements as the target. Two things in it are
directly relevant.

First, they apply auxiliary RDM losses at *several* layers at once - "after both
max pooling layers and the fully connected layer" - which is precedent for our
multiloss arm rather than for the blends.

Second, they decay the auxiliary weight to zero across training,
`alpha_t = alpha_0 * (1 - t / t_max)`, so the auxiliary term steers early and
then gets out of the way. Our curves peak by step 100-400 and decline after, so
this looked like the matching fix. Implemented as `--lambda-decay linear` and run
for 3 seeds at layer 12, against the existing constant-lambda arm:

| metric | constant 0.7 | decay 0.7 -> 0 | diff | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| best checkpoint | +0.0787 | +0.0716 | -0.0071 | 0.170 |
| endpoint, step 800 | +0.0718 | +0.0604 | -0.0114 | 0.366 |

So it is mildly worse on both, not significantly, and it does not reduce the late
decline - the gap between best and endpoint is *larger* under decay (0.0113
against 0.0069).

**The useful part is the diagnostic.** Over the last 400 steps the RSR arm's
absolute Neither score falls by essentially the same amount either way:

- decay arm, lambda falling 0.35 to 0.00:  -0.0157
- constant arm, lambda 0.70 throughout:    -0.0162

Removing the RSR gradient entirely changes the decline by 0.0005. So the late
decline is **not** RSR overtraining or sustained RSR pressure - it happens just
as fast with no RSR signal at all. What is left is continued MLM training
drifting the representation away from the structure RSR installed. The control
arm is roughly flat over the same window, so it is specifically the *installed*
structure that erodes, not general degradation.

That reframes the early stopping question. Early stopping is not protecting us
from too much RSR; it is protecting us from too much training. The implication is
that the lever worth pulling is training length or learning-rate schedule, not
the RSR weight - and it also means a fixed short run is a defensible choice
rather than a convenient one.

Runs: `results/bert_continued_*_2026082{5_234026,6_003811,6_013329}.csv`.

## Layer weighting, both readings of it

"Weight the layers" can mean two different mechanisms, and they give different
answers. Both are now tested at n = 3 on the strict pool, same recipe throughout
(weighted 0.7, 800 steps), all four arms evaluating on the last layer so the
numbers are directly comparable.

**Pool layers into one vector, take one loss** - the scalar-mix arms.
**Apply a separate loss at each of several layers** - the multiloss arm, one
soft-Spearman term each at L7, L9 and L12, equally weighted
(`--rsr-loss-layers 7,9,12`).

Neither in RSR, gain over the matched control, paired against layer 12 by seed:

| arm | mean gain | vs layer12 | p | per-seed diffs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| layer12 | +0.080 | - | - | |
| multiloss | +0.070 | -0.010 | 0.288 | -.012, -.020, +.003 |
| learnedmix | +0.044 | -0.036 | 0.071 | all negative |
| frozenmix | +0.040 | -0.040 | 0.036 | all negative |

So multiple losses *tie* plain layer 12 - not significant, and one seed goes the
other way - while averaging the layers is significantly worse. The distinction
matters: averaging appears to actively blur the read-out, whereas adding RSR
terms at other layers is roughly neutral, with the layer-12 term doing the work.

Neither version beats reading the final layer, so the layer direction is closed
under both interpretations. Worth presenting as two results rather than one,
since only the averaging version is a clear negative.

Multiloss is also worse on the supervised partitions (One -0.008 against layer12's
+0.029, Both -0.044 against -0.029), consistent with everything else: the gains
concentrate on unsupervised pairs.

Implementation note: `target_vectors_multi` pools every requested layer out of a
single forward pass, and the weights are normalised to sum to 1 so the total RSR
magnitude - and therefore the meaning of rsr_lambda - is unchanged from a
single-layer run. Runtime is about 80 min a job against 62 for the single-layer
arms.

A useful calibration fell out of it. The control arm never touches the RSR code
path, so multiloss and layer12 controls should be identical at a fixed seed; they
differ by about 0.003. That is run-to-run GPU nondeterminism, and it puts a floor
of roughly 0.003 under any effect we quote - about 25x below the +0.07 to +0.09
we report.

## Layer read-out: what Vulic et al. actually recommend

The relevant layer study is Vulic, Ponti, Litschko, Glavas & Korhonen (2020), EMNLP
7222-7240, "Probing Pretrained Language Models for Lexical Semantics". Their
layer method is not a learned mix - it is `AVG(L<=n)`, a *uniform* mean over
layers 0..n, swept over n and compared against using layer n alone. It is a
probing study on a frozen model: no training anywhere.

Their two claims are that averaging over a subset of the bottom layers beats any
single layer, and that averaging over *all* layers hurts.

Both were tested here with `--probe-layers` on the strict pool, which now reports
`Ln` and `AVG(L<=n)` side by side. The result splits by partition:

| partition | best single | best prefix avg | winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| all pairs | L8 = 0.5230 | AVG(L<=9) = 0.5417 | prefix, +0.019 |
| neither | L9 = 0.5543 | AVG(L<=12) = 0.5510 | single, -0.003 |

On all pairs their finding replicates cleanly, peak at n = 9 and a decline after,
so all-layer averaging does mildly hurt. On Neither neither half holds: the
prefix curve climbs monotonically to n = 12 with no interior peak, and never
catches plain layer 9.

Since Neither is the only partition that survives the corpus check, there is no
read-out gain available on the number we actually report. That is a stronger
basis for dropping the layer direction than the scalar-mix arms were, because it
tests the configuration the study endorses rather than the one it warns against.

Two checks fall out of it. Probe L12 Neither = 0.5152 matches the layer12 arm's
vanilla 0.515, and probe L9 = 0.5543 sits where the mix arms' vanilla read-out
was (0.556) - so the probe and the training runs agree.

Care with the earlier claim: the scalar-mix arms weighted all 13 layers, which is
the setting Vulic et al. report as harmful. "We tried layer weighting and it
doesn't help" was therefore too broad. The defensible version is that no read-out
tried so far - single layer, learned mix, fixed probe-weighted mix, or uniform
prefix average - improves RSR on the unsupervised pairs.

Still untested: applying the RSR loss at several layers at once with separate
weights, rather than pooling layers into one vector and applying one loss. If
that is what was meant, it remains open.

## The training corpus was doing more work than we thought

The original pipeline filters its Wikipedia sentences and we didn't, so we rebuilt the pool two
ways and re-ran the 5-seed experiment on each. Same recipe throughout (weighted
0.7, 800 steps, layer 12), only the sentence pool changes.

- **original** - `AllCombined.txt`, which turns out to be *Simple English*
  Wikipedia (179 MB, 277k articles, 29M tokens), no filtering
- **cleaned** - same source, the original filters applied after the fact: terminal
  punctuation, drop matches where the target only appears capitalised
  mid-sentence, drop duplicates. 158k -> 131k sentences
- **strict** - re-harvested from the full enwiki dump with the original rules built in:
  lowercase-token matching, one sentence per article per target, 5-100 words.
  177k sentences, and only 11 targets needed the always-capitalised fallback
  against 158 in the cleaned pool

RSR minus the matched MLM control, 5 seeds, paired t-test:

| Partition | original | cleaned | strict |
|---|---|---|---|
| All pairs | +0.018 (p=.017) | +0.003 (p=.57) | +0.029 (p=.00005) |
| Both in RSR | +0.007 (p=.58) | -0.028 (p=.0025) | -0.009 (p=.46) |
| One in RSR | +0.002 (p=.75) | -0.010 (p=.23) | +0.011 (p=.087) |
| **Neither in RSR** | **+0.053 (p=.00002)** | **+0.037 (p=.00026)** | **+0.070 (p=.00010)** |

**Neither in RSR is the only thing that holds.** Positive, significant, and
better in 5 of 5 seeds in every corpus. Everything else changes sign or
significance depending on which sentences the words were embedded in, so
all-pairs and Both should not be reported as results.

The reason the effect size moves is worth stating carefully. RSR's *absolute*
score on Neither is almost identical everywhere - 0.614, 0.607, 0.606 - while
vanilla BERT swings 0.550 / 0.558 / 0.515. Full Wikipedia is harder for vanilla
BERT than Simple English (median sentence 24 words against 18, far more varied),
and RSR pulls it back to the same place regardless. So the delta is mostly a
statement about where the baseline sits, not about RSR getting better or worse.
Quote the corpus whenever quoting the number.

Runs: `results/bert_continued_*_20260817_*` (cleaned) and `_20260817_21*` through
`_20260818_02*` (strict). Scripts: `experiments/contextual/audit_harvest_quality.py`,
`filter_sentence_pool.py`, `harvest_sentences_strict.py`.

Two things neither pipeline handles. Words that are always capitalised in real
use (iPod, SIM card, Christmas tree) are destroyed by the lowercase-token rule -
the original `[a-z]+` regex would starve them too, silently. Our version falls back to a
case-insensitive pool per target and records it in the manifest. And the strict
pool still holds ~1% exact duplicates, because Wikipedia genuinely reuses text
across articles, which one-sentence-per-article doesn't prevent.

## GPT-2: the last layer is not a usable read-out

Ported RSR to GPT-2 (`gpt2_rsr_continued_pretraining.py`). It imports the BERT
script rather than reimplementing anything, so supervision, SimLex partitioning,
sentence pool and the soft-Spearman loss itself are the same code. Only the
model and its anchor change: the anchor is causal LM instead of MLM, keeping the
form `(1-lambda) * anchor + lambda * RSR`.

Before spending GPU time, a reduced CPU probe (150 pairs, 3 sentences per word)
flagged the last layer as unusable. The full probe then confirmed it on all 999
pairs with 20 sentences per word:

| layer | all | both | one | neither | mean cos | sd cos |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.412 | 0.291 | 0.601 | 0.405 | 0.721 | 0.063 |
| 6 | **0.529** | 0.429 | 0.608 | 0.540 | 0.749 | 0.083 |
| 7 | 0.526 | 0.409 | 0.599 | **0.543** | 0.760 | 0.083 |
| 11 | 0.412 | 0.219 | 0.486 | 0.453 | 0.897 | 0.041 |
| 12 | **0.161** | **0.023** | 0.279 | 0.180 | **0.994** | **0.006** |

**Correction to the CPU probe.** It reported layer 12 at -0.103, i.e. negatively
correlated. On the full set it is +0.161, so the "negative correlation" claim was
an artefact of 150 pairs and 3 sentences per word and should not be repeated. The
qualitative conclusion is unchanged and better supported: at layer 12 every
cosine sits at 0.994 +/- 0.006, the space has collapsed to a needle, and
all-pairs falls to 0.161 against 0.529 six layers below.

**The comparison with BERT is the interesting part.** BERT's probe peaks
mid-stack too (all-pairs at 8, Neither at 9, Both at 7, One at 2) and GPT-2 is
similar low down (all at 6, Neither at 7, Both at 6, One at 2). They diverge
entirely at the top: BERT dips at 11 and recovers at 12 to 0.480, while GPT-2
falls off a cliff to 0.161. GPT-2's best layer, 0.529, is actually *higher* than
BERT's final layer, so GPT-2 is not a worse lexical model. It just does not keep
that information at the top, because the top is committed to next-token
prediction. This is Ethayarajh (2019), measured on our own pairs.

**A consequence for what "the same experiment" means.** BERT's layer 12 happens
to be a serviceable lexical read-out; GPT-2's is not. So "read from the last
layer" matches the layer NUMBER across the two models while mismatching what that
layer does, and nominal parallelism and functional parallelism are two different
experiments. Both should be run and reported as such.

**The design consequence.** The BERT experiments established that the
best-probing layer is not the best layer to attach RSR to: layer 7 read better
than 12 but gained less, +0.027 against +0.053. So layer 6 cannot be assumed
correct for GPT-2 either. The comparison should run at both layer 12 and a
middle layer, mirroring the layer 7 vs 12 arm.

That also sets up a sharper version of the BERT finding. There, a better
starting point produced a smaller gain. GPT-2 gives a far more extreme test of
the same claim, since layer 12 starts at -0.10 and layer 6 at +0.55. If the BERT
pattern holds, layer 12 should gain *more* despite starting worse. If it does
not, the "better start, smaller gain" claim is BERT-specific and should be
narrowed.

Span finding verified at 100% over 300 sampled words (space-prefixed form
catches 293, bare 7); mean 1.56 sub-word pieces per word, so pooling is a real
choice. Raw probe output: `notes/gpt2_mini_probe_raw.txt`.


## Templates vs real sentences, and what evaluation context hides

The two original RSR arms differ by one flag in that code, `use_templates`
(`samplers.py:107` in the original), which swaps every harvested sentence for the fixed null
context "The word is X.". The original `rsr_all` rises on every partition; its
`rsr_targetwords` collapses early. Evaluation context is a SECOND, independent
flag there (`eval_simlex.py`, default `bare` = the word alone), so a moving curve
could be the training changing or the measurement changing, and the original figure cannot
separate them.

Ran both training regimes in our pipeline against the same matched MLM control,
3 seeds, 2000 steps, strict corpus, scoring every checkpoint in three evaluation
contexts. Neither partition at step 2000, minus control:

| eval context | sentence-trained | range | template-trained | range | p (paired) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sentences (ours) | **+0.0449** | [+0.039, +0.053] | **+0.0190** | [+0.017, +0.020] | **0.037** |
| bare (original default) | +0.1288 | [+0.052, +0.185] | +0.1162 | [+0.048, +0.154] | 0.29 |
| template | +0.1842 | [+0.082, +0.283] | +0.2232 | [+0.114, +0.295] | 0.15 |

**The result: bare evaluation cannot tell the two training regimes apart.**
Under contextual evaluation, template training delivers 42 percent of what real
sentences deliver, and the difference is significant across seeds. Under bare
evaluation the two are statistically indistinguishable (p = 0.29). A measurement
that returns the same answer for a training scheme that works and one that
largely does not is not measuring training quality.

Note which numbers are trustworthy. The contextual column has a range of 0.014
across seeds; the bare and template columns range over 0.13 and 0.20. Bare words
and null frames are far outside the distribution the model trains on, so those
probes are degenerate and inherit large run-to-run variance. **The absolute
out-of-context figures should not be quoted as effect sizes.** The claim above
rests on a within-seed comparison on the stable measurement.

**The template arm optimises the objective better and generalises worse.** Mean
RSR training loss 0.1501 against 0.1907, reproducible to four decimals across
seeds (0.1497, 0.1498, 0.1508). Deterministic vectors with no context noise are
an easier fitting problem. It still lands at under half the payoff.

**Separately: continued MLM training destroys out-of-context structure.** The
control alone, all pairs, step 0 to 2000: sentences 0.480 -> 0.498 (flat), bare
0.167 -> 0.120, template 0.503 -> 0.250. Continued training halves the template
probe while leaving contextual evaluation untouched. RSR prevents this. That is a
cost of continued pre-training we had not measured, and it is invisible unless
you probe out of context.

**What this does and does not settle about the disagreement.** It accounts for
the original RED line: `rsr_all` trains on templates and, on the default bare evaluation,
is scored close to where it was trained, on a measurement blind to the
difference. It does NOT account for the ORANGE line. The hypothesis going in was
that the original real-sentence collapse was a measurement artefact; it is not, because
our real-sentence arm is healthy under all three evaluation contexts. The
collapse remains unexplained and the candidates are unchanged: RoBERTa vs BERT,
layer 5 vs 12, alternating vs weighted losses, morphological augmentation.

Open question: what `--eval_mode` were the plotted runs launched with. If
`bare`, the two arms were not measured on the same footing.

partitions, band = range across 3 seeds).

## GPT-2: the result transfers, smaller on SimLex and sharper as a dissociation

The main pipeline, run on an autoregressive model. Supervision, the SimLex
partition, the sentence pool and the soft-Spearman loss are the same code,
imported from the BERT script rather than reimplemented. Only the model and its
anchor change: causal LM in place of masked LM, keeping
`(1-lambda) * anchor + lambda * RSR` at lambda 0.7. Three seeds, 2000 steps,
matched CLM control, read-out at layer 6.

**Neither is the only partition that improves, and every partition is

| | BERT | GPT-2 |

Two things worth saying carefully. **The SimLex gain is about a third of BERT's,

### The layer had to be chosen differently, and this is not a free parameter

The full probe (results/gpt2_layer_probe_20260908_005631.csv) found GPT-2's
final layer degenerate: all-pairs 0.161 with cosines at 0.994 +/- 0.006, a space

the same pairs. So BERT's final layer is a serviceable lexical read-out and
GPT-2's is not, and "read from the last layer" matches the layer NUMBER across

## Post-specialisation reaches the unsupervised words, and harms them

Our retrofitting result reads exactly +0.0000 on unsupervised pairs, which is
arithmetically forced and, as noted, is the motivating problem of Vulic et al.
(2018) rather than our discovery. Post-specialisation is their remedy, so it is
the more demanding baseline and the one a reviewer is most likely to name.

Implemented in three stages: retrofit the supervised words with the same k-NN
graph and the same best k as before; fit a residual two-layer map from original
to retrofitted vectors on those words only, holding out a tenth to select the
checkpoint; apply the map to the whole vocabulary. 690 of 3,213 words had never
been touched by the specialisation.

| change vs stock BERT | all | both | one | neither |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| retrofitting | -0.2233 | +0.1901 | -0.0639 | **+0.0000** |
| post-specialisation | -0.0463 | +0.1326 | -0.0985 | **-0.0258** |
| RSR | +0.053 | -0.008 | +0.045 | **+0.091** |

**The three methods separate cleanly.** Retrofitting cannot reach the
unsupervised words. Post-specialisation reaches them and moves them the wrong
way. RSR reaches them and helps.

The map is not failing to fit: held-out MSE is 0.009. It generalises as a
function and the transformation it learned is simply wrong for these words. The
mechanism is visible in what it was fitted to. Retrofitting compresses the
supervised region from 0.64 to 0.90 mean cosine, so a map trained to reproduce it
learns to compress, and compressing words whose geometry was already appropriate
flattens the distinctions among them.

What post-specialisation *does* fix is the global damage: all-pairs recovers from
-0.223 to -0.046, because propagating one transformation to every word restores
the scale agreement between regions that retrofitting destroyed. That is a real
strength and worth reporting rather than burying.

**Scope, stated carefully.** This is post-specialisation applied to OUR
retrofitting stage, not a reimplementation of their system, which used a
max-margin objective over a specialisation model with antonym constraints our
supervision does not contain. The claim is correspondingly narrow: learning to
propagate *this* specialisation to unseen words does not help them. A
specialisation that distorted the supervised region less might propagate better,
and explicit retrofitting (Glavas & Vulic 2018) learns its function from the
constraints directly rather than from an already-specialised table, so it is not
subject to the same objection. Neither is tested.

Script: `experiments/rsr_pretraining/eval_postspec_baseline.py`.
Raw output: `notes/postspec_raw.txt`.

## Loose ends

**Report vs code, in the original repo.** Three places the write-up and the code
disagree. None of them change the conclusions but they'd want fixing before
anything goes out:

- report says `rsr_targetwords` uses 4 edge-pairs per batch for VRAM reasons;
  `train.py:211` uses 24 for both RSR modes
- report says `rsr_targetwords` restricts target words to those overlapping
  SimLex; there's no such filter, the sampler gets the full `training_data` in
  both modes. `use_templates` is the only coded difference between the two.
- `eval_pereira_rsa.py`'s `load_or_simulate_brain_matrix` quietly swaps in a
  random matrix when the beta CSVs are missing. Since `data/` is gitignored,
  anyone cloning the repo hits that path immediately and gets numbers that look
  completely normal. Would raise instead.

**Still open.** Real-sentence RSR breaks in the original setup and works in ours. Untested
differences: read-out layer (5 vs 12), learning rate (1e-5 vs 5e-5), and the
supervision pool.

**Also worth checking at some point.** The original splits each word's 60 sentences 30
train / 30 eval; our BERT pipeline serves the same pool to both. Expected
overlap is only ~2 of the 20 eval sentences per word so it's probably immaterial,
and it can't touch the Neither bucket at all (those words are never RSR targets).
